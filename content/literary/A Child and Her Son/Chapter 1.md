
There is an upper limit to the amount of tears a human could ever cry in their lifetime, and I already ran out of my quota before I was 7.

Mother should be one’s first sight of a woman. But my mother, for me, becomes every sight of woman. I see her in every woman and feel eager to fall into her arms and lie on her tender bosom, and yet the same kind of aversion toward her rises, destroys, and forces me to turn my head away. I was fed her breast milk until I was 3. Is that a way to nourish or to control? Is that the reason why I cried day and night until utter exhaustion hit after she left me behind at 4? Is that a suggestion of love or the adoration of a pet? Am I her extension reaching toward what she left undone, or an independent entity apart from her? I am pretty sure that none of these questions has ever popped up in her mind, since she was and still is such an ignorant, shallow, and blind person. But who cares as a 4-year-old kid? A child solely desires joy and love, and those who lack joy and love as children remain children. I am a child. Now I don’t know whether it’s a good thing or a bad thing. But it’s okay. It is what it is.

My mother has become an indifferent stranger now, for time and the wounds accompanying its passage are irreversible, and I have set out to find love and joy in other ways. Yet still, whenever my past floods into my mind, I somehow enjoy the pain and return to being the weeping boy again.

As I walked along the empty path across the woods near our campus, a girl seemingly of the same age appeared in front of me. She had such grand eyes, in sharp contrast with my mother’s small, narrow ones. Her name was Wilky. I didn’t mean to bring my mother up or compare them intentionally. It’s just that she nonsensically occupied some parts of me and seemed to stick to me as an internalized standard—she became my every sight of woman. Sometimes the childlike attributes my mother possesses would register directly in her eyes: hesitation, worry, skepticism, innocence, anger, disappointment. Those moments remind me of the self-evident fact that she is also but a child. She never even attempted to pretend. I love those pure, honest, authentic, naked registrations of sentiment as much as I love children, yet I can barely stand that ignorance as the other side of the coin. Her presumed, blinding conviction that she knows me irritates me all the time, to such a degree that I let go of the rationality suppressing the repulsion, unleash my resistant yelling at her, and then hatred and disgust take shape and twist my face into that of another being. It only happens between her and me. It might not be a proper private present for a mother, but it suffices as a lifelong curse for a son.

However, it must be noted that not all children act naturally to get what they desire. Some of them gain attention by performing, and my mother is sometimes one of them. She would always pull out her phone to film the dishes on the dining table and me, intentionally delivering dull, caring remarks: “See, we have four dishes today; babe, do you like them? Eat as much as you want, so you will grow up faster”—in a superficial, bragging, discernibly pretentious tone, with awkwardly forced delight in her smile. The sense of being objectified enrages me: it seems to me that I am nothing more than a mirror reflecting her glorious, selfless motherly love or a container for her cheap love—anything but a real person.

I always have an indifferent, nonchalant facial expression when I walk, so that others cannot speculate about my mind—or maybe I just try to do the opposite of my mom. So people usually don’t say hi to me. But Wilky did. Presumably she ignored my head when it was in her line of sight. She saw a person without a face. And it is exactly this that makes me like her. My mother, by contrast, would always endeavor to find something on my face that she could comment on or ask about: Why do you look so tired, baby? Why don’t you laugh, honey? Your dark circles are getting heavier; have you had enough sleep? I always answered with “Yes” or “No” absentmindedly and tried to hold my anger back. Yet it disturbed me so much when I found myself asking Wilky the same type of questions from some day onward. Are they common concerns between loved ones? If so, does it mean I didn’t love my mother, and that is why it irritated me? If not, does it mean I am so affected by my mother’s shadow?

I have not hugged her for more than 10 years; or, put another way, I only hugged her for 4 years. A hug is such a remote concept, as remote as streams flowing in the Sahara Desert are to southern China. A hug gives a sense of home, and I have long been homeless. One cannot hug oneself; therefore, pathetically, no home can be found within oneself. I missed my home 13 years ago as I missed my instinctive attachment to my mother then.

I missed her. She would buy me all the toys I wanted despite being short of money; she would keep me company forever as long as I asked her to; she would get some live fish from the wet market and go to the lake nearby, murmuring Buddhist scriptures, and bestow on them a bright new life at their own disposal. I still remember the shrill sounds as that flesh rubbed against the plastic bag: the irony that, in spite of their efforts to see and live independently, in the end their seeming success in breaking through was nothing more than a dull ritual designed by my mother, saddens me even now.

Now all the toys she bought me are long gone, all those liberated fish are dead, and all the Buddhist scriptures she taught me have faded, yet my primitive, childish wish remains clearly and intensely felt: My mother is to persist as long as I ask her to. As I lay in Wilky’s bosom, pondering all these things, my nose started to sting. As usual, no tears could possibly come out. It’s expected, since there is no way for me to become that 7-year-old boy again, and so it is for my mother to be my mother again. That was like a million years ago.
