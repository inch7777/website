## Subgroups

**Definition 2.1.** Let $(G, *)$ be a group. A nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is called a **subgroup** of $G$ if $H$ is closed under the operation $*$ and, with the induced operation, $H$ itself forms a group. We denote this by $H \leq G$.

**Theorem 2.2 (Subgroup Criterion).** A nonempty subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup if and only if for all $a, b \in H$, we have $a * b^{-1} \in H$.

**Theorem 2.3 (Finite Subgroup Criterion).** If $H$ is a finite nonempty subset of a group $G$ and $H$ is closed under the group operation (i.e., $ab \in H$ for all $a, b \in H$), then $H$ is a subgroup of $G$.

 ~={red}$Proof 2.3$=~
~={red}We first prove $H$ itself is a group. =~
~={red}Associativity: For all $a,b\in H$, $a,b \in G$, therefore $(ab)c=a(bc)$ holds.=~
~={red}Identity: Let $a \in H$, considering sequence $a^k$, where $k \in Z_{+}$. Since H is finite, there must exist a minimum $k_{0}$ so that $a^{k_0}=e$ or $a^k$ would become a infinite sequence and thus H is infinite. Hence, $e \in H$ =~
~={red}Inverse: As mentioned above, for each  $a \in H$, there must exist a minimum $k_{0}$ so that $a^{k_0}=e$. Then $a^{k_{0-1}}a=e \to a^{-1}=a^{k_{0-1}}$.=~
~={red}Therefore $H$ itself is a group.=~
~={red}Given that it is an nonempty subset of $G$ and is closed under the group operation, by definition 2.1 it is the subgroup of $G$.=~

**Theorem 2.4.** Let $H \leq G$. Then:

(a) The identity element of $H$ is the identity element of $G$.

(b) The inverse of an element in $H$ is its inverse in $G$.

~={red}This is also shown in proof 2.3.=~

**Definition 2.5.** The **trivial subgroup** of a group $G$ is the subgroup $\{e\}$ consisting only of the identity element. A subgroup $H$ is called a **proper subgroup** if $H \neq G$.

**Example 2.6.** The even integers $2\mathbb{Z}$ form a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.

**Example 2.7.** The set of rotations in the dihedral group $D_n$ forms a subgroup.

$D_{n}= \{ r,s|r^n=s^2=e , rs=sr^{-1}\}$

**Example 2.8.** The set of permutations of a set $A$ that fix a particular element $a \in A$ forms a subgroup of $S_A$.

**Theorem 2.10.** The intersection of any collection of subgroups of a group $G$ is again a subgroup of $G$.

**Definition 2.11.** Let $S$ be a nonempty subset of a group $G$. The **subgroup generated by $S$**, denoted $\langle S \rangle$, is the intersection of all subgroups of $G$ containing $S$. Equivalently, $\langle S \rangle$ consists of all finite products of elements of $S$ and their inverses. If $G = \langle S \rangle$, we say that $S$ **generates** $G$.

**Exercise 2.13.** Let $H \leq G$ and $K \leq G$. Prove that $HK = \{hk \mid h \in H, k \in K\}$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if $HK = KH$.

$Proof:$

## Cyclic Subgroups

**Definition 2.14.** Let $G$ be a group and $a \in G$. The **cyclic subgroup generated by $a$** is
$\langle a \rangle = \{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\},$
where $a^0 = e$, $a^n = a * a * \cdots * a$ ($n$ factors) for $n > 0$, and $a^n = (a^{-1})^{-n}$ for $n < 0$.

**Definition 2.15.** A group $G$ is **cyclic** if $G = \langle a \rangle$ for some $a \in G$. Such an element $a$ is called a **generator** of $G$.

**Example 2.16.** $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is cyclic; both $1$ and $-1$ are generators.

**Example 2.17.** $(\mathbb{Z}_n, +_n)$ is cyclic; $1$ is a generator.

**Example 2.18.** The group of $n$th roots of unity in $\mathbb{C}$ is cyclic under multiplication.

**Definition 2.19.** The **order** of an element $a \in G$, denoted $|a|$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $a^n = e$. If no such $n$ exists, $a$ has **infinite order**.

**Theorem 2.20.** If $a$ has finite order $n$, then:
$$\langle a \rangle = \{e, a, a^2, \ldots, a^{n-1}\},$$
and these $n$ elements are distinct.

$Proof:$
By definition 2.19 𝑛  is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $a^n = e$. 
Therefore,
1) for all $k \in \mathbb{Z},k<n,$ $a^{n+k}=a^k$.  By definition2.14 $\langle a \rangle = \{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}\text{ should equal to} \{e, a, a^2, \ldots, a^{n-1}\},$
2)  Suppose, for contradiction, there exists $a^m=a^p, \text{where m is not euqal to p}, m,p \in \mathbb{Z}, m<p<n$. Then by multipling $a^{n-m}$ on both side we deduce $e=a^{n-m+p}=a^{p-m}$, where $0<p-m<n$ and $a^{p-m}=e$. It's contradict to that $n$ is the order of $a$. Hence there n elements are distinct.
Thus, we are done.

**Theorem 2.21.** For any $a \in G$, $|a| = |\langle a \rangle|$.

$Proof:$
By Theorem 2.20, If $a$ has finite order $n$ i.e. $|a|=n$, then:
$\langle a \rangle = \{e, a, a^2, \ldots, a^{n-1}\}$ consists n distinct elements.
Thus, For any $a \in G$, $|a| = |\langle a \rangle|$.

**Theorem 2.22.** If $a^k = e$, then $|a|$ divides $k$.

**Theorem 2.23 (Order of a Power).** Let $a$ have order $n$. Then for any integer $k$:
$$|a^k| = \frac{n}{\gcd(n, k)}.$$

$Proof:$ 
Let $k=pt,n=qt,t=gcd(n,k), gcd(p,q)=1$.
Since by Theorem 2.21, for any $a \in G$, $|a| = |\langle a \rangle|$, $|a^k| = |\langle a^k \rangle|$.
We now consider $\langle a^k \rangle=\{ a^{pt}, a^{2pt},\dots,a^{qpt}\dots \}$
Since $gcd(p,q)=1$, the smallest positive integer h such that $(a^k)^h=e$ is $q$.
Therefore, $|a^k| = |\langle a^k \rangle|=q= \frac{n}{\gcd(n, k)}.$
Remark: gcd is the "viable" step size for k to finish the "run" at length n. By "viable", we mean that it actually contributes reaching the multple of n. 


**Corollary 2.24.** $a^k$ generates $\langle a \rangle$ iff $\gcd(n, k) = 1$.

$Proof:$
When $\gcd(n, k) = 1$,
applying Theorem 2.23, $|a^k| = \frac{n}{\gcd(n, k)}=n=|a|$
By Theorem 2.21, $|a| = |\langle a \rangle|=|a^k| = |\langle a^k \rangle|$
Since by definition $a^k \in \langle a \rangle$, $\langle a^k \rangle \subset\langle a \rangle$ and both of them have order n (have the same number of elements),
$\langle a \rangle=\langle a^k \rangle$.

When $a^k$ generates $\langle a \rangle$.
$\frac{n}{\gcd(n, k)}=|a^k| =|\langle a \rangle|= n$
Therefore $gcd(n,k)=1$

## Subgroups of Cyclic Groups

**Theorem 2.28.** Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

$Proof:$
Take a subgroup $H$ of a cyclic group $G=\langle a \rangle$ with order n.
The elements of $H$ must all have the form $a^k, k\in \{ 0,1,2,\dots n-1 \}$ .
In non-trivial case, i.e. the subgroup is not $\{ e \}$, which is cyclic, there must exist $1\leq k_{0} \leq n-1$ such that $k_{0}$ is the smallest among all the $k$.
Then $H$ must also contain $a^{2k_{0}},a^{3k_{0}},\dots a^{|a^k_{0}|}=e$, where $|a^k_{0}|= \frac{n}{gcd(n,k_{0})}$.
That is, $\langle a_{k_{0}}\rangle \subset H$.
Suppose there exists $a^t$ in $H$ where $t \text{ is not a multiple of } k_{0}$.
Suppose $gcd(t,k_{0})=m \leq k_{0}$. By Bézout's lemma, there exists $p,q \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $pt+qk_{0}=m$.
Therefore, similarly, there exists $a^{pt+qk_{0}=m}=a^m$ . Since $k_{0} \leq m$ and $m \leq k_{0}$, then $m=k_{0}$, which contradicts the supposition $t \text{ is not a multiple of } k_{0}$.
Hence, for all the $a^k$ in $H$, $k \text{ is the multiple of } k_{0}$, that is $H \subset \langle a^{k_{0}}\rangle$. 
Therefore, in conclusion, $H = \langle a^{k_{0}}\rangle$.

When $G=\langle a \rangle$ is  infinite. 
Take a subgroup $H$ consisting of $a^k, k\in \mathbb{Z}$, there must exist $a^{k_{0}}$ such that $k_{0}$ is the smallest among all the $k$. Then $\langle a^{k_{0}}\rangle \text{(which is also infinite)} \subset H$. 
Similarly:
Suppose there exists $a^t$ in $H$ where $t \text{ is not a multiple of } k_{0}$.
Suppose $gcd(t,k_{0})=m \leq k_{0}$. By Bézout's lemma, there exists $p,q \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $pt+qk_{0}=m$.
Therefore, similarly, there exists $a^{pt+qk_{0}=m}=a^m$ . Since $k_{0} \leq m$ and $m \leq k_{0}$, then $m=k_{0}$, which contradicts the supposition $t \text{ is not a multiple of } k_{0}$.
Hence, for all the $a^k$ in $H$, $k \text{ is the multiple of } k_{0}$, that is $H \subset \langle a^{k_{0}}\rangle$. 
Therefore, $H= \langle a^{k_{0}}\rangle$

**Theorem 2.29.** Let $G = \langle a \rangle$ be a cyclic group of order $n$.

(a) For each divisor $d$ of $n$, there is exactly one subgroup of order $d$, namely $\langle a^{n/d} \rangle$.

$Proof:$
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists another subgroup of order $d$ generated by $a^p,p<n,dp \text{ is not equal to n}$
$\langle a^p \rangle=\{a^p, \dots, a^{dp} \}$, and $a^{dp}=e=a^{kn}, k\in \mathbb{Z_{+}},k \text{ is not equal to 1}$.
$dp=kn \implies p=k\frac{n}{d}$. 
Then $\langle a^p \rangle=\langle a^{k\frac{n}{d}}\rangle=\{a^{k\frac{n}{d}}, \dots, a^{dp} \}$, which is a subgroup of $\langle a^{n/d} \rangle$.
Since both of them have order $d$, $\langle a^p \rangle=\langle a^{n/d} \rangle$.
Therefore, the supposition fails: there only exists exactly one subgroup of order $d$, namely $\langle a^{n/d} \rangle$.

(b) The subgroups of $G$ are precisely $\langle a^d \rangle$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$.

$Proof:$
On the one hand, by (a), we deduce that all $\langle a^d \rangle$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$ are subgroups of $G.$

On the other hand, suppose, for contradiction, there exists another subgroup(cylic, by theorem2.28) $\langle a^m \rangle$, where $m$ is not a divisor of $n$, $gcd(m,n)=t$, and $t$ does not equal to $m$ or m would be a divisor of $n$. 
By Bezout's lemma, there must exists $p,q \in \mathbb{Z}$, $pm+qn=t$.
Then in $\langle a^m \rangle$, there must exists $a^{pm+qn}=a^{pm}\cdot e=a^{pm}=a^t$, where $t \leq m$. 
However, since the subgroup is generated by $m$, $t\geq m$.
Therefore, $t=m$, which contradicts to the supposition.
Thus, there does not exist another subgroup(cylic, by theorem2.28) $\langle a^m \rangle$, where $m$ is not a divisor of $n$. 

So the subgroups of $G$ are precisely $\langle a^d \rangle$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$.

(c) If $H = \langle a^k \rangle$, then $|H| = n / \gcd(n, k)$.

**Theorem 2.30.** Let $G = \langle a \rangle$ be infinite cyclic. Then every nontrivial subgroup is infinite cyclic, and the subgroups are precisely $\langle a^k \rangle$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.

$Proof:$
Apparently all the $\langle a^k \rangle$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ are infinite subgroups of $G = \langle a \rangle$.
By theorem2.28, all of the subgroup of a cyclic group are cyclic.
Thus, they must all have the form $\langle a^k \rangle$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.
Therefore, the subgroups are precisely $\langle a^k \rangle$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.


**Theorem 2.31 (Subgroup Lattice of Cyclic Groups).** For a cyclic group $G = \langle a \rangle$ of order $n$, the subgroups form a lattice under inclusion, ordered by divisibility: $\langle a^d \rangle \subseteq \langle a^e \rangle$ iff $e \mid d$.

$Proof:$
If $e \mid d$, $d=ke,k\in \mathbb{Z}$, $\langle a^d \rangle=\langle a^{ke} \rangle \subseteq \langle a^e \rangle$.
If $\langle a^d \rangle \subseteq \langle a^e \rangle$, then $a^d\in \langle a^e \rangle \implies a^d=a^{ke},k\in \{ 1,2,\dots e-1 \}$. Therefore, $e \mid d$.

Thus,  $\langle a^d \rangle \subseteq \langle a^e \rangle$ iff $e \mid d$.

## Generators of Cyclic Groups

**Definition 2.36.** The **Euler phi-function** $\varphi(n)$ is the number of integers $k$ with $1 \leq k \leq n$ such that $\gcd(k, n) = 1$.

**Theorem 2.37.** A cyclic group $G=\langle a\rangle$ of order $n$ has exactly $\varphi(n)$ generators.

By theorem 2.23, $|a^k| = \frac{n}{\gcd(n, k)}$. The generator of $G$ should have order $n$. Then that requires $gcd(n,k)$ to be $1$. 
By definition 2.36,  $\varphi(n)$ is the number of all integers $k$ with $1 \leq k \leq n$ such that $\gcd(k, n) = 1$, and thus the number of generators of $G$.

**Example 2.38.** $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ has $\varphi(12) = 4$ generators. They are $1, 5, 7, 11$.
